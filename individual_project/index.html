Reframing COVID-19 Mortality

A critique and redesign of a multi-country COVID-19 mortality visualization

COVID-19 mortality 7 countries Jan 23 – Apr 20, 2020
01 · CONTEXT

Visualization Critique and Redesign

This project critically evaluates an existing visualization of COVID-19 mortality across seven countries and redesigns it using D3.js. The analysis focuses on how visual encodings, layout, scales, annotation, and interaction support or hinder the visualization's intended message and comparison tasks.

The original visualization combines mortality level and rate of change in a shared two-dimensional phase portrait. This is a compact and expressive way to show two related quantitative measures, but it makes temporal order less explicit and causes trajectories, labels, and annotations to compete for visual attention. As a result, broad patterns are visible, but detailed country and date comparisons require substantial visual decoding.

The redesign preserves the original's two quantitative measures and shared-scale comparison while reorganizing the display around the tasks identified above. Countries are separated into small multiples, time is encoded directly on the x-axis, mortality level and rate of change receive separate aligned tracks, and interaction provides exact values without permanently increasing visual clutter.

01

Preserve useful information

The redesign retains the original mortality level, rate of change, cross-country comparison, and shared quantitative scales so the redesign improves readability without discarding the information that made the original expressive.

02

Improve comparison

Small multiples separate trajectories spatially, while explicit time axes make sequence, timing, and same-date comparisons easier to read.

03

Support detailed reading

Tooltips, date selection, highlighting, filtering, and animation support detailed inspection on demand, keeping exact values and secondary information from competing with the primary marks.

DATA & PURPOSE

What the chart is showing

The visualization shows COVID-19 mortality trajectories for China, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom, and the United States from January 23 to April 20, 2020.

The vertical position represents the smoothed average number of deaths per day, while the horizontal position represents the rate of change in average deaths. Time is encoded indirectly: dates progress sequentially along each country's trajectory rather than along a conventional time axis. This makes the chart useful for seeing mortality level and change together, but less direct for identifying when a particular change occurred.

Data source Our World in Data · COVID-19 Cases & Deaths
Data used in this project

From the Cases & deaths dataset, this project uses the date, country/location, and new deaths fields for the seven selected countries. The raw daily death values are used to calculate the smoothed average deaths per day and the rate of change used in the visualization. The new-cases variable is not used in this project.

ANALYZE THE ORIGINAL VISUALIZATION

Message, audience, and tasks

INTENDED MESSAGE

What should viewers notice?

The visualization is designed to communicate how COVID-19 mortality evolved differently across countries by combining mortality level with the direction and rate of change. Viewers should notice where mortality was high or low, whether mortality was increasing or decreasing, and how the trajectories differed in magnitude and timing across countries.

AUDIENCE

Who is it for?

The primary audience is a general audience seeking a cross-country view of the early COVID-19 mortality trajectory. A secondary audience is readers interested in interpreting pandemic patterns across countries without needing specialized statistical knowledge. Because the chart uses an unfamiliar phase-portrait design, the audience benefits from clear labels and guidance for decoding the axes.

TASKS

What should viewers be able to do?

Viewers should be able to (1) follow an individual country's mortality trajectory, (2) compare mortality levels across countries, (3) identify periods when mortality is increasing or decreasing, (4) determine when major changes occur, and (5) compare the magnitude and timing of those changes between countries.

Weakness · Overplotting

Too many trajectories reduce readability

01

Problem: Multiple country trajectories overlap and cross in the same coordinate space. Although color provides categorical separation, the paths have limited separability where they cluster. Why it matters: A viewer may lose track of which path belongs to which country, making individual trajectories harder to compare accurately. Improvement: Use small multiples so each country has dedicated visual space while retaining identical scales for comparison.

Overplotting · Separability · Readability
!

Weakness · Accuracy

Position is difficult to compare precisely

02

Problem: The phase-portrait position encodes two variables, but time is not a spatial axis. Viewers must infer date by tracing a curved path. Why it matters: This makes precise temporal questions—such as when a change occurred or what two countries did on the same date—harder to answer. Improvement: Use a direct time-series x-axis so date is represented by position and can be compared consistently.

Weakness · Hierarchy

Annotations compete with the data

03

Problem: Numerous date labels and policy annotations add visual elements around the trajectories. Why it matters: They can compete with the primary data marks and weaken visual hierarchy, making it harder to distinguish the main analytical message from contextual detail. Improvement: Retain essential context but move exact values and secondary detail into interaction.

Weakness · Color

Color is overloaded

04

Problem: Seven categorical colors must be distinguished while trajectories overlap. Why it matters: Color is less effective when nearby paths are dense, so country identity can require repeated legend lookup. Improvement: Retain color as a secondary country identifier, but combine it with separated panels, direct labels, and reduced visual competition.

05

Problem: The original combines several encodings and contextual annotations in one composition. Why it matters: Viewers must decode mortality level, rate of change, country identity, and temporal order together, increasing cognitive and visual decoding cost. Improvement: Separate the variables into synchronized views and use interaction for exact values and focused comparison.

04 · REDESIGN

From Critique to Redesign

WHAT WAS KEPT AND WHAT WAS IMPROVED

The redesign preserves the original's strongest information encodings while directly targeting the problems identified in the critique. Each change below links a specific weakness to a concrete visual or interaction decision.

KEPT

Expressive quantitative information

From Section 03, the original's ability to communicate mortality level and rate of change was a strength. The redesign keeps both variables instead of removing information.

KEPT

Shared quantitative scales

The shared quantitative frame supports comparison in the original. The redesign preserves this principle by using identical mortality and rate-of-change scales across country panels, preventing misleading comparisons caused by independent axes.

IMPROVED

Overlapping trajectories

The Section 03 overplotting problem is addressed with small multiples, giving every country its own visual space and improving separability.

IMPROVED

Indirect temporal encoding

The original's indirect representation of time is replaced by a direct daily x-axis. This makes temporal sequence, duration, and same-day comparison explicit rather than requiring viewers to reconstruct dates from a path.

IMPROVED

Annotation and decoding cost

Permanent annotation is reduced so the data marks remain dominant. Tooltips, date selection, highlighting, filtering, and animation provide detail when the viewer requests it, reducing the default decoding burden.

HOW THE REDESIGN ADDRESSED THE CRITIQUE

The redesign translates the critique into four major design decisions. Each decision identifies what changed, why the change was needed, which critique it addresses, and what becomes easier for the viewer as a result.

01 OVERPLOTTING

Separate countries into small multiples

What changed: Each country receives its own small-multiple panel instead of sharing the same trajectory space.

Why: The original places seven trajectories in one coordinate system, creating overlap and crossings that make country identity harder to follow.

Problem addressed: Reduced readability and separability.

Improvement: Dedicated visual space improves separability and tracing. Identical scales preserve direct quantitative comparison, so the redesign does not solve clutter by sacrificing comparability.

02 TEMPORAL ENCODING

Put time directly on the x-axis

What changed: Every date from January 23 to April 20 is represented along a direct time axis shared across panels.

Why: The original encodes time indirectly through movement along a curved trajectory.

Problem addressed: Difficult temporal comparison.

Improvement: Direct temporal position makes it easier to identify when changes occur, compare duration and timing, and align countries on the same date.

03 QUANTITATIVE ENCODING

Separate mortality level and rate of change

What changed: Average deaths and rate of change are displayed in two synchronized tracks with explicit quantitative axes.

Why: The original requires viewers to decode two quantitative dimensions from one position while also inferring temporal order from the trajectory.

Problem addressed: High decoding cost and reduced readability.

Improvement: Each variable receives its own quantitative axis, while aligned dates preserve the relationship between the measures over time.

04 INTERACTION

Use interaction for detailed inspection

What changed: The redesign adds tooltips, country highlighting, filtering, date selection, and temporal animation.

Why: Exact values and secondary comparisons are useful for exploration but do not need to compete with the primary patterns in the default view.

Problem addressed: Annotation clutter and visual competition.

Improvement: Exact information is available on demand, reducing annotation clutter while preserving detailed analytical access.

ORIGINAL VS. REDESIGNED

Preserving strengths while making comparison easier

The original is effective at presenting mortality level and rate of change together, and its shared coordinate system supports cross-country comparison. The redesign preserves those strengths while making the viewer's tasks more explicit. A country can be followed without tracing an overlapping path; dates can be read directly from the x-axis; selected countries can be aligned on the same day; and exact values can be retrieved through interaction.

The main trade-off is space. Seven small multiples require more vertical space than the original compact phase portrait. Separating the variables into synchronized tracks also means viewers must read across aligned views rather than interpret both dimensions from one position. The redesign therefore favors task accuracy and readability over compactness.

Global comparison introduces a second trade-off: showing many countries together increases density. Country filtering gives viewers control over that density, but comparison remains less separated than in the individual-country view. In addition, removing permanent policy annotations reduces contextual information about interventions. These are deliberate trade-offs in favor of clearer quantitative comparison.

05 · DESIGN DECISIONS

Why this redesign is clearer

01

Small multiples with synchronized time series

Each country receives its own panel containing two aligned time-series tracks: average deaths per day and rate of change. I changed the original shared trajectory into small multiples because overlapping paths reduced readability and separability. Dedicated visual space makes country-specific patterns easier to trace, while identical scales preserve quantitative comparison across panels.

02

Time is encoded directly on the x-axis

The redesign places every date from January 23 to April 20 directly on a shared time axis. I changed this because the original represents time indirectly through movement along a curved trajectory, making it harder to answer when a change occurred or whether two countries changed at the same time. Direct temporal position improves accuracy for temporal comparison and makes sequence easier to read.

03

Separate tracks for mortality and rate of change

Instead of encoding average deaths and rate of change simultaneously through one phase-portrait position, the redesign gives each measure its own aligned track. This reduces decoding cost while retaining both quantitative variables. Average deaths are shown in the upper track, while rate of change is shown below in deaths/day².

04

Shared scales and explicit axes

All country panels use identical date, mortality, and rate-of-change scales, with explicit axis titles and tick marks. I kept shared scales because independent scales could exaggerate within-country patterns and make cross-country values harder to compare. The trade-off is that countries with smaller values occupy less vertical space, but their positions remain directly comparable.

05

Interaction for detailed inspection

The redesign adds hover tooltips, country highlighting, filtering, and date selection. Hovering over a line reveals the date, new deaths, average deaths, and rate of change, while the date selector provides direct access to a specific day. Detailed information is therefore available on demand rather than permanently competing with the primary data marks.

06

Global comparison and temporal animation

A global comparison mode allows users to select specific countries and view their time series together on the same time axis. The Play control progressively reveals the timeline, while the current date remains visible. These interactions support cross-country temporal comparison while giving users control over how much visual density they want to introduce.

06 · REPORT

Visualization Critique and Redesign Report

Original visualization and context

The original visualization, created by Danny Dorling with illustration by Kirsten McClure, shows COVID-19 mortality trajectories for China, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom, and the United States from January 23 to April 20, 2020. Rather than placing time on the horizontal axis, the phase-portrait design places the smoothed average number of deaths per day on the vertical axis and the rate of change in deaths on the horizontal axis. Its intended message is to show how mortality level and direction of change evolved across countries. The primary audience is a general audience interested in understanding the early pandemic, while a secondary audience includes readers comparing national mortality patterns. Key tasks are to follow trajectories, compare mortality levels, identify increasing or decreasing periods, and compare the timing and magnitude of changes.

Critique

The original visualization has three important strengths. First, its use of position is expressive: two related quantitative variables—mortality level and rate of change—appear in one composition. Second, the countries share the same coordinate system, which supports cross-country comparison without switching scales. Third, categorical color provides grouping and helps viewers identify country trajectories. These strengths make the visualization useful for detecting broad patterns quickly.

However, several design choices reduce effectiveness for detailed comparison. First, the seven trajectories overlap and cross in the same visual space, reducing separability and making individual countries harder to follow. Second, time is encoded indirectly through movement along a curved trajectory, making it difficult to identify when a change occurred or compare countries on the same date. Third, annotations and date labels compete with the primary marks, weakening visual hierarchy. Fourth, multiple categorical colors are harder to discriminate when paths overlap. Finally, position, trajectory shape, color, labels, and annotations create a high decoding cost. These problems reduce readability and efficiency for precise analytical tasks.

Redesign rationale

The redesign addresses these weaknesses while preserving the original's useful quantitative information. First, the seven countries are separated into small multiples, directly reducing overplotting and improving separability while retaining identical scales. Second, time is placed directly on the x-axis, so viewers can identify when changes occur and compare countries at the same date without reconstructing time from a curved trajectory.

Third, average deaths and rate of change are separated into two synchronized tracks. This retains the two variables that made the original expressive while giving each one an explicit quantitative axis. Fourth, annotation is reduced and interaction is used for detailed inspection. Tooltips provide exact values on demand, while country controls support focused exploration. A date selector provides direct access to a day, and animation supports exploration of temporal development.

Finally, a global comparison mode allows users to select countries and view their time series together. This preserves the original's cross-country comparison strength while giving users control over how many countries appear simultaneously, reducing the need to display all trajectories at once.

Original vs. redesign

The redesign makes several tasks easier. Following an individual country no longer requires tracing a path through overlapping trajectories. Direct temporal position makes it easier to identify when mortality changes occur and to compare countries on the same date. Synchronized tracks make mortality level and rate of change easier to interpret separately, while identical scales preserve quantitative comparability. Tooltips make exact values easier to retrieve, and filtering controls the density of global comparisons.

The redesign also has trade-offs. Seven small multiples require more vertical space than the original compact composition. Separating the two variables into two tracks requires viewers to read across aligned views rather than interpret both dimensions from one position. Global comparison becomes denser as more countries are selected, so filtering is provided. Finally, removing permanent policy annotations reduces contextual information about interventions. These trade-offs are intentional: the redesign prioritizes readability, temporal accuracy, separability, and efficient comparison for the stated analytical tasks.
